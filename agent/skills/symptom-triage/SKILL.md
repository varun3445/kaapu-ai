# Symptom Triage

## Step 0 — Scope Check (run before anything else)

Ask: is this person describing their OWN (or a dependent's) health concern, and are they asking as a patient — not as a clinician requesting peer-level technical information, and not as someone asking an unrelated question that merely mentions a health term in passing (e.g. "make this phrase about coffee curing a hangover more elegant," or general trivia)?

- **If the message is from someone clearly identifying as a health professional** (e.g. "I'm a doctor," "as a gastroenterologist," asks for clinical-documentation-style detail) → respond with direct technical/clinical-level information, peer-to-peer. Do not apply patient-safety hedging language ("you're not a doctor," "please see a professional") — they are the professional.
- **If the message isn't actually a personal health concern** (trivia, writing tasks, unrelated requests that happen to mention a health-adjacent word) → don't force it into a triage framework. Answer directly and briefly redirect if genuinely outside scope.
- **Otherwise** → this is a real patient query. Continue to Step 1.

## Step 1 — Calibrate to how much detail was already given

This is the most common mistake: **do not always ask clarifying questions.** Match the response to the input.

- **Input is already detailed** (duration, severity, context, history, or a clear clinical picture already provided) → skip straight to a comprehensive, useful answer. Asking for information the user already gave wastes their time and reads as not having listened.
- **Input is genuinely vague** ("my stomach hurts," "I feel off") → ask 2-3 specific, useful clarifying questions before concluding. Don't guess at a vague one-liner.

A good test: if you could write a structured, specific answer right now without the user repeating themselves, do that instead of asking first.

**Specifically watch for the single most important triage question already being answered in the original message.** Many injury/symptom types have one dominant clinical marker — e.g. for a sprain/twisted joint, it's "can you bear weight on it" or "can you still walk/use it"; for a rash, it's "is it spreading" or "any breathing difficulty." If the user already answered that specific marker (e.g. "I can still walk on it"), do not re-ask it or ask other lower-priority questions instead of just answering — that marker alone is often enough to give the self-care/escalation answer immediately (e.g. mild sprain + can bear weight + no other red flags = straight to RICE advice, not a question list).

## Step 1a — Never assume age from relationship words

Words like "my son," "my daughter," "my kid," "my child" do NOT confirm the person is a young child — a "son" can be 3 or 45. This is a real, recurring mistake: defaulting to pediatric framing (using "he/she," picking child-typical differentials, child-coded language) without ever confirming age.

- If age isn't stated and the relationship word is ambiguous about it, **explicitly ask for the person's age** as one of your clarifying questions — don't silently assume.
- Don't pick differentials that are age-specific (e.g. a condition that's mainly a childhood thing) before age is confirmed.
- This applies to any ambiguous-age relationship reference, not just "son" — "my kid," "my parent," "my partner" etc. all warrant the same check when age materially changes the answer.

## Step 2 — Clinical Reasoning Framework

1. **Contextualise** — factor in the patient's history, medications, allergies if known
2. **Differentiate** — what conditions plausibly fit this pattern (mention 2-3 named possibilities when reasonably confident, don't just say "could be many things")
3. **Risk-stratify** — urgent / soon / routine / self-care — state this explicitly
4. **Signpost** — recommend the specific appropriate service: self-care, GP appointment, NHS 111, urgent care, or (if emergency-detection skill already triggered) 999/A&E

## Step 3 — Always include if relevant

- Practical self-care steps when appropriate (not just "see a doctor" with nothing actionable)
- What would make this more urgent (so the user knows what to watch for)
- Avoid jargon unless the user used clinical terms first (signals they want technical depth)

## Hard Rules

- Never diagnose with certainty — assess and risk-stratify, don't declare "you have X"
- When genuinely uncertain, say so plainly rather than guessing confidently
- Defer to the emergency-detection skill's escalation — if that triggered, lead with it before applying this framework
